I am in my senior year of high school. The summer before it, I was sure that I was going to do math for the rest of my life. Now I am amazed by the latest proofs of GRH, Navier-Stokes, etc., and at the same time unsure about my future prospects.

As with many of us, I started liking math at a specific point. For me, it was the calculus series by Grant Sanderson on the YouTube channel 3b1b. At that time, I had just turned 16 and was fascinated by simple derivative rules; I tried to understand them, not memorize them. For me, it was a kind of spiritual ritual: daily rewatches, mental struggle, and a bit of practice.

Throughout my freshman year of high school, I understood why I love math in general. It is a kind of sandbox: you invent rules and then study their consequences. There are no empirical observations; one statement just follows from a bunch of others, and this logicality is deeply satisfying.

What I see now, given AI advancements, is that math is shifting from an invented science to an empirical one: we hand conjectures to a theorem-proving machine, power it with data centers, and observe a result. I don't want that; I am not interested in this. I want to get to the result through a sequence of steps, motivated by a reason, applying methods from similar problems or coming up with new ones. AI does those things for us, providing us with the answer. In the end, math may become more like biology. We will be able to understand the consequences of a theorem, but how and why will be hidden unless someone spends an enormous amount of time getting to them. What if we don't even understand the consequences?

In this case, a few questions arise. What do we value as a society and mathematical community: consequences, or how and why, or something else? If no human can understand and apply a theorem, but AI can, do we value this theorem or not? If this theorem is applied in cancer research and humans can benefit from it, does that make it more valuable? I believe most of us would answer yes to the last one. And this is the reason why the math the younger generation was promised is disappearing. If understanding is the most fun part of math, and AI is a gazillion times faster and smarter, what is the point of me — and all the young people going into mathematics — doing it at all?

To be fair, this is already the case in some ways: theorems are proven by mathematicians, and researchers use the consequences without understanding the whole proof. The distinction lies in the fact that those theorems can be understood by a human, since a human could prove them, and that at least one human actually does or did understand them.

The joy of why is disappearing in math, at least for me personally.
