A year ago I tried to learn the 3x3 using the beginner's method and stopped after the first two layers. As far as I remember, I didn't like the learning curve: progress was slow and not really rewarding, as I was targeting completion of a cube as my goal.

This September I decided to try again, approaching it differently. Since my last attempt I had leveled up in algebra, so I knew that the Rubik's Cube is essentially a non-commutative group of actions.

Thus, my approach differed from the previous one. I didn't care about speed, only about understanding what commutators (a sequence of actions that affects a local part of the cube while keeping the rest of it approximately the same) do and how we combine them to get to the goal. As a result, I learned the beginner's method in less than four days. I believe it felt easier because of the prior knowledge and my new attitude towards the process.

Solving a 3x3 comes down to juggling three commutators:
1. Rotation of a selected corner
2. Cycling corners
3. Cycling edges

All the steps in between rely on visual intuition and appear with practice.

For me, the most fun part of cubing is not doing it as fast as possible but understanding what commutators do to the cube and using them accordingly.

After the 3x3 I got a 5x5. It felt more interesting: the complexity grows, and now you need to manage centers and pair edges to be able to solve it. This builds the habit of fixing what you broke while building a bar. This part is not present in the 3x3 at all. Also, the 3x3 is way too rigid: you don't really have space to experiment, and solving it means learning three algos. When it comes to the 5x5, spatial intuition and variability become a thing.

Now my collection consists of three MoYu cubes (3x3, 5x5 and 9x9) and a no-name Pyraminx. For me, cubing is not about speed (even though I still care about it) but about being able to translate a complex puzzle into manageable constructs that solve it for you.
